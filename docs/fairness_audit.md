# Fairness audit

Segmented from the 80-ticket validation run in `evaluation/results/validation_01/`. The tables are generated by `analysis/fairness_audit.py` into `docs/fairness_tables.md` and can be regenerated against any finished run; this file is the reading of them.

The governance condition is **under five points of cross-group variation**. On the headline measure — the share of tickets answered without a person — the system fails that condition on every dimension examined:

| Dimension | Spread in automatic-answer rate |
|---|---:|
| Customer tier | 30.4 points |
| Region | 31.2 points |
| Ticket length | 45.5 points |
| Language fluency | 14.2 points |

Reporting only that would be accurate and useless. A gap in outcomes is unfair when the system causes it, and ordinary when the groups send different tickets. Those two cases are separated by looking at each stage of the pipeline independently, because a system that discriminated would degrade at the stage where it reads the text.

## The pipeline itself is flat

Across every dimension, retrieval, classification and citation resolution are indistinguishable between groups once the confidence intervals are read:

- Retrieval hit rate sits between 93% and 100% in every group, with overlapping intervals everywhere.
- Classification accuracy sits between 86% and 100%, again overlapping.
- Citation resolution is 95% to 100% everywhere.

So the variation is not in how the system reads or answers a ticket. It appears only at the routing decision, which is where ticket content is supposed to change the outcome.

## Tier, region and length are explained by content

For these three, the escalations are dominated by `protected_category`, and the groups that escalate more are the groups that send more protected-category tickets.

The clearest case is ticket length. Short tickets are answered automatically 55% of the time against 73% for medium ones, a gap of 18 points. But 36% of short tickets are labelled must-not-auto-respond against 11% of medium ones. A ticket under twenty words is frequently an unclear request, and the policy says an unclear request goes to a human. The same pattern holds for tier: 26% of standard-tier tickets carry a protected-category intent against 7% of business.

This is corroborated structurally rather than only statistically. `src/route.py` takes a ticket, a classification and the retrieved passages, and reads none of `customer_tier`, `customer_region` or `language_fluency`. There is no path by which those attributes could influence the decision.

Two caveats. The region and enterprise-tier cells are small — Latin America is 7 tickets and enterprise is 8 — and at that size a single ticket moves the rate by more than the five-point threshold, so neither supports a conclusion in either direction. And "caused by content" is not the same as "acceptable": standard-tier customers do wait longer for an answer, whatever the reason. Whether that is tolerable is a question for the client, not one this audit can settle.

## Language fluency is different, and this is the finding

The Governance Framework predicts that retrieval will do worse on non-fluent English, because the phrasing stops matching the documentation. **That did not happen.** Retrieval hit rate on non-fluent tickets was 100%, marginally above the 98% on fluent ones. The most likely reason is a decision made during retrieval tuning for an unrelated purpose: every indexed chunk has its article title prepended, which was done because customers describe symptoms in different words from the documentation's headings. That change appears to have absorbed the effect the framework warned about.

But a disparity does show up, and unlike the others it is not explained by content:

| | fluent | non_fluent |
|---|---:|---:|
| Tickets | 61 | 19 |
| Must-not-auto-respond by label | 18% | 16% |
| Classification accuracy | 90% | **95%** |
| Mean stated confidence | 0.907 | **0.863** |
| Escalated below the confidence threshold | 0 | **2** |

The protected-category base rate is essentially identical, so content does not account for the gap. Non-fluent tickets are classified *more* accurately, yet the model reports *lower* confidence on them, and **all three tickets in the entire run scoring below the 0.80 threshold were non-fluent**. The confidence threshold then converts that under-confidence into escalation.

That is a real mechanism, and it is the system's doing rather than the ticket's. The model is reading non-native phrasing as uncertainty when its own answers show it is not uncertain. Because the threshold acts on stated confidence, miscalibration that correlates with a protected attribute becomes unequal treatment directly.

The honest size of the effect is small: 2 tickets out of 19. The interval on that rate is wide and it would not survive a significance test. It should be reported as a signal to watch with a named mitigation, not as a demonstrated harm — but it should be reported, because the direction is specific, the mechanism is identifiable, and a larger run could confirm it cheaply.

## What follows

The calibration problem is not confined to fluency. Across the whole run, correctness is not monotonic in stated confidence: 0.80 and 0.95 both scored 100% while 0.90 scored 87%. The governance condition **"confidence calibrated within five points" therefore cannot be claimed as met**, and the report should say so plainly rather than omit the condition.

Candidate mitigations, in the order their cost is justified:

1. **Monitor the fluency gap in the confidence band directly** — a counter on sub-threshold escalations split by fluency, which the decision log already has the columns to support. Cheap, and it turns a 19-ticket signal into something that accumulates evidence in production.
2. **Stop relying on the confidence threshold for safety.** It accounts for 2 of 23 escalations, and the grounding checks and protected-category rule do the real work. Treating confidence as a weak signal rather than a gate removes the pathway by which this disparity reaches the customer at all.
3. **Do not raise the threshold.** The obvious reflex is to tighten it; here that would escalate correctly-classified non-fluent tickets at a higher rate than fluent ones and make the disparity worse while appearing more cautious.

Decisions left open for the report, which are judgement rather than measurement: whether a 30-point tier gap explained by content is acceptable to the client; and whether the fluency signal warrants delaying deployment or warrants a monitored rollout.
